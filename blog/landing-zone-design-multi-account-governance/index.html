Landing Zone Design: Getting Account Structure Right

Cesar A. Nogueira
April 22, 2025 · 12 min read

Why the account topology decision is effectively permanent

Every cloud environment starts the same way: someone spins up a single account, deploys a workload, and it works. At that scale, a flat structure is not a mistake — it's the correct amount of process for the problem. The trouble is that this structure doesn't stay a convenience. Within a year, that one account has IAM policies scoped to specific resource ARNs, CI/CD pipelines hardcoded to a project ID, VPC peering connections tying it to three other accounts, and a compliance auditor who now treats the account boundary as the security boundary. What began as an arbitrary container has become load-bearing infrastructure.

This is the part teams underestimate: fixing account structure later is not a configuration change, it's a migration. Moving a production workload into a new account means re-issuing every credential and service principal that touches it, re-establishing network routes and peering, replaying data into new storage resources or executing a controlled cutover, and re-pointing every pipeline, monitoring integration, and DNS record. None of this can usually be done with a flag flip — it's live infrastructure migration, with the attendant downtime risk, rollback planning, and weeks of validation. Doing this for one workload is a project. Doing it for a hundred, because the whole environment grew flat, is a multi-quarter program.

The thesis of this article follows directly from that asymmetry: the account and subscription topology you choose on day one — along with the guardrail model that enforces it — determines how much of that migration pain you pay later, and whether you pay it at all. Get the boundaries right before the first production workload lands, and the environment scales by repeating a pattern. Get it wrong, and you scale by accumulating debt that eventually forces the exact disruptive rebuild this article is about avoiding.

The pattern AWS, Azure, and GCP all converge on

Despite marketing differences, the three major providers converge on the same structural idea for multi-account governance: a top-level root construct that owns policy, a centrally governed platform layer that provides shared services and guardrails, and isolated workload containers that inherit those guardrails automatically rather than re-implementing them.

On AWS, this is AWS Organizations paired with Control Tower, which "offers a straightforward way to set up and govern an AWS multi-account environment, following prescriptive best practices," orchestrating Organizations, Service Catalog, and IAM Identity Center to build what AWS calls a landing zone. Control Tower's own documentation is explicit about the mechanism: it applies controls, "sometimes called guardrails," to prevent environments from drifting from established practice, and recommends this orchestration layer specifically once you're managing "more than a handful of accounts."

On Azure, the equivalent is Management Groups layered over subscriptions, described in the Cloud Adoption Framework as consisting of two components: a "platform landing zone" that is "the centralized foundation that establishes governance, security, and shared resources," and application landing zones that sit above it and consume those shared services.

On Google Cloud, the same shape appears as the resource hierarchy: organization at the root, folders as the governance-inheritance layer (often mapped to business units or environments), and projects as the workload isolation unit, with IAM and Organization Policy inherited down the tree. Google's own architecture framework documents this same organization-folder-project structure as the basis for applying governance and access controls consistently across an estate, rather than per resource.

The vendor terms don't line up one-to-one — a Management Group is not exactly a folder, and an AWS Organizational Unit (OU) is not exactly a folder either — but the governance intent is identical in all three: policy attaches once, high in the hierarchy, and every account, subscription, or project created underneath inherits it without anyone having to remember to apply it manually. This is why the design decisions in the rest of this article — how to draw boundaries, where to attach guardrails, how to centralize shared services — transfer across clouds almost without translation. If your organization is multi-cloud, you can apply the same boundary logic in each provider's native hierarchy rather than inventing a new model per cloud.

Drawing your account and subscription boundaries

The practical question is what determines account boundaries. Four axes recur across most landing zone designs, and the skill is knowing when to combine them and when to keep them separate:

  • Environment — production, staging, development. This is almost always a hard boundary; environments should never share an account.
  • Workload or application — a distinct product or service with its own lifecycle, on-call rotation, and blast radius.
  • Business unit — useful when cost allocation, budget ownership, or organizational autonomy needs to map to the account structure itself, not just to tags.
  • Compliance scope — PCI-DSS, HIPAA, or data-residency requirements that need an isolated boundary regardless of who owns the workload.

A common pattern consistent with AWS's own guidance is one account per workload, per environment — so a payments service gets a payments-prod account and a payments-dev account, not a shared payments account with environments distinguished by naming convention. The AWS whitepaper on multi-account strategy recommends "using several accounts to separate your workloads, rather than relying on a single account," and frames that separation as helping you optimize across "most of the AWS Well-Architected Framework pillars, including operational excellence, security, reliability, and cost optimization." The reasoning is concrete rather than aesthetic — an account boundary gives you a hard IAM trust boundary (a compromised credential in one account cannot silently pivot into another), a clean cost-allocation unit (no tagging discipline required to answer "what does this workload cost"), and a blast-radius container (a misconfigured security group or an exhausted service quota in one account cannot cascade into a sibling workload).

The failure mode in the opposite direction is over-fragmentation: hundreds of near-empty accounts created for every microservice, environment, or team, each requiring its own baseline configuration, its own budget alarms, its own access reviews. This produces the same operational tax as a flat structure, just distributed — the effort spent standing up and reconciling accounts often exceeds the effort saved by isolating them. A workable rule of thumb: create a new account when a workload has an independent deployment lifecycle, an independent compliance or data-sensitivity profile, or an independent team that needs autonomous IAM control — not simply because it is a separate microservice repository. If two services deploy together, share an on-call rotation, and have identical compliance requirements, they can usually share an account.

The guardrail model: preventive controls at the platform layer

Guardrails split into two categories that belong at different points in the hierarchy. Preventive guardrails make an action impossible — a denied API call, a rejected deployment. Detective guardrails let the action happen and flag it afterward — a compliance finding, an alert, a scheduled remediation. Preventive controls belong at the platform layer, attached high enough in the hierarchy that every account created underneath inherits them automatically; detective controls can live closer to the workload, since they're about visibility rather than hard denial, and workload teams often need to tune their sensitivity.

On AWS, preventive guardrails are implemented as Service Control Policies (SCPs) attached to an OU. A simplified illustration of an SCP denying accounts from leaving the organization and restricting deployments to approved regions looks like this — treat it as a fragment to adapt, not a production-ready policy: a real region-restriction SCP needs a longer list of exempted global/account-management services (billing, health, IAM, STS, and others) or it will silently break services that aren't scoped to a region.

{
  "Version": "2012-10-17",
  "Statement": [
    {
      "Sid": "DenyLeaveOrganization",
      "Effect": "Deny",
      "Action": "organizations:LeaveOrganization",
      "Resource": "*"
    },
    {
      "Sid": "DenyOutsideApprovedRegions",
      "Effect": "Deny",
      "NotAction": [
        "iam:*",
        "organizations:*",
        "route53:*",
        "cloudfront:*",
        "support:*"
      ],
      "Resource": "*",
      "Condition": {
        "StringNotEquals": {
          "aws:RequestedRegion": ["us-east-1", "eu-west-1"]
        }
      }
    }
  ]
}

The Azure equivalent is an Azure Policy definition with an allowedLocations effect, assigned at the Management Group level. Like the SCP above, this is a simplified illustration rather than something to deploy verbatim — Microsoft's actual built-in "Allowed locations" policy also excludes resources whose location is global and resources of type Microsoft.AzureActiveDirectory/b2cDirectories from the deny check, so that global-scoped resources (Traffic Manager profiles, certain DNS/CDN/Front Door and policy metadata resources) aren't blocked org-wide:

{
  "properties": {
    "displayName": "Allowed locations for resources",
    "policyType": "Custom",
    "mode": "Indexed",
    "parameters": {
      "listOfAllowedLocations": {
        "type": "Array",
        "metadata": {
          "displayName": "Allowed locations",
          "description": "The list of locations that can be specified when deploying resources"
        }
      }
    },
    "policyRule": {
      "if": {
        "allOf": [
          {
            "field": "location",
            "notIn": "[parameters('listOfAllowedLocations')]"
          },
          {
            "field": "location",
            "notEquals": "global"
          },
          {
            "field": "type",
            "notEquals": "Microsoft.AzureActiveDirectory/b2cDirectories"
          }
        ]
      },
      "then": {
        "effect": "deny"
      }
    }
  }
}

In practice, prefer assigning Azure's built-in "Allowed locations" policy definition directly rather than hand-rolling this JSON — it's maintained by Microsoft and already carries the correct exclusions.

The point of both snippets isn't the specific denial — it's where they attach. Bound to an OU or a Management Group rather than to individual accounts, every new account or subscription created under that node inherits the restriction the moment it's provisioned, with no step where someone has to remember to apply it. That inheritance is the actual value of the hierarchy; a flat account structure has nowhere to attach a policy that automatically covers accounts that don't exist yet.

Centralizing identity, logging, and network as shared services

Once the hierarchy exists, three capabilities should be built once, centrally, rather than duplicated in every workload account: identity federation, audit logging, and network connectivity. Duplicating an identity provider integration per account multiplies the attack surface and the operational burden of rotating credentials or revoking access during an incident. A dedicated identity or "core" account holding the SSO integration (IAM Identity Center, Azure AD/Entra ID tenant-level configuration, or a GCP identity federation project) means access changes propagate from one place, and workload accounts only ever consume roles, never define their own trust relationships from scratch.

Logging follows the same logic, with one additional requirement: audit-log immutability. Logs describing what happened in a workload account should not be alterable by that same account's administrators — otherwise a compromised or rogue account owner can cover their own tracks. The standard pattern is a dedicated log-archive account (AWS), a centralized Log Analytics workspace in a platform subscription (Azure), or a centralized logging project with aggregated sinks (GCP), where the workload account has write-only permission to ship logs out, and no permission to modify or delete them once they land.

Network connectivity between the platform and workload accounts is handled through hub-and-spoke topologies — AWS Transit Gateway, Azure Virtual WAN, or GCP Network Connectivity Center — where the hub owns shared network appliances (firewalls, egress proxies, DNS resolvers) and workload accounts or VPCs peer into it rather than each provisioning their own internet gateway and NAT infrastructure. (GCP's Shared VPC is a related but distinct construct: it lets multiple projects attach to and use a single VPC's network administered from a host project, which centralizes IAM control over one network, but it doesn't itself provide the multi-VPC transit routing that Transit Gateway or Virtual WAN offer — Network Connectivity Center is the closer analog for that routed hub-and-spoke pattern.) This keeps egress policy enforcement in one place and makes network segmentation between workloads a routing-table decision rather than something each team has to reimplement.

Automating account provisioning instead of hand-rolling it

None of the guardrail or shared-services design matters if new accounts don't reliably get it applied. This is what account-vending automation solves: AWS Control Tower's Account Factory (or Account Factory for Terraform for teams that want the process in version control rather than the console), the Azure Landing Zone accelerator, and GCP project-factory patterns built on Terraform (with Infrastructure Manager or Config Connector as the GCP-native options) all do the same job: turn "create a new account" into a pipeline execution rather than a manual checklist.

The case for this is less about convenience and more about guarantee. A manual account-creation checklist — even a well-documented one — degrades under time pressure; someone skips the SCP attachment or forgets to wire up the centralized logging sink during an incident-driven rush to stand up a new environment. A Terraform module invocation, or a Control Tower Account Factory customization, cannot skip a step; the guardrails are attached as part of the same apply that creates the account. The cost is real setup investment upfront — building and testing the module, defining the OU/folder/Management Group templates, wiring the pipeline that invokes it — but that cost is fixed, while the cost of manual account creation compounds linearly with every account created afterward, and non-linearly with every guardrail that gets missed.

Untangling a sprawling single-account environment without a rebuild

Teams that already run a single sprawling account are not stuck rebuilding from scratch, but the migration needs sequencing to avoid the exact disruption this article opened with. Start by introducing the organizational hierarchy — the management account, the OU or Management Group structure, the guardrail attachments — without moving any workloads yet. This gets the platform layer live and tested in isolation.

Next, migrate by environment before migrating by business unit or workload. Moving non-production workloads into their new accounts first is lower-risk and surfaces the real operational gaps — broken pipeline assumptions, hardcoded account IDs, missing cross-account IAM roles — while the blast radius of a mistake is contained to a dev or staging environment.

Production workload migration should come last, and it deserves the most caution: it's the step most likely to cause customer-visible downtime, and the one where credential re-issuance and network re-routing carry real risk if something is missed. Sequence it with a tested rollback path — ideally a parallel-run period where the workload operates from both the old and new account before cutover, with traffic shifted gradually rather than all at once — and treat it as its own project with its own change window, not a line item in a broader "move to Organizations" initiative.

This staged migration is, concretely, the disruption the day-one thesis warns about. Teams doing it are paying the retrofit cost directly. The argument for correct topology at the outset isn't theoretical — it's the observation that every team going through this migration would tell you to design the hierarchy first if they were starting over.

A day-one checklist for landing zone design

Before the first workload account exists, resolve these decisions:

  • Boundary axis: will accounts split by environment, workload, business unit, or compliance scope — and where do you combine axes to avoid over-fragmentation?
  • Guardrail attachment point: which OU or Management Group holds the preventive policies (region restriction, org-exit denial, mandatory logging), so new accounts inherit them without manual steps?
  • Shared-services scope: which capabilities — identity federation, audit logging, network egress — live centrally, and what's the immutability guarantee on the log archive?
  • Provisioning automation: is account creation a pipeline invocation (Control Tower Account Factory, Landing Zone accelerator, Terraform project factory) or a manual process that can silently skip a guardrail?

This checklist is a minimum defensible baseline — enough to survive a security or compliance review and to make cost isolation possible from day one — not a complete architecture. Review it against the AWS Well-Architected Framework, the Azure Well-Architected Framework, or the Google Cloud Well-Architected Framework for the fuller set of pillars — reliability, performance, sustainability — that a mature landing zone eventually needs to address.

What to do next

If you're starting fresh, stand up the organization-level hierarchy and attach the guardrail policies before creating a single workload account — don't let a "temporary" account become the pattern everything else copies. If you're already running flat, start the staged migration outlined above with non-production workloads, and use that migration to build and test the account-vending pipeline you'll need before touching production. Either way, treat the Terraform or IaC module that provisions new accounts as the actual deliverable of this work — it's what makes the guardrail model durable rather than a one-time console exercise that erodes the next time someone needs an account in a hurry.
