Landing Zone Design: Multi-Account Governance from Day One

Cesar A. Nogueira
April 22, 2025 · 11 min read

When ad hoc multi-account sprawl stops working

Most multi-account environments start the same way: someone spins up a new AWS account or Azure subscription for a project, gives it a name that made sense at the time, and moves on. Six months later there are forty of these, no two named the same way, no consistent tagging, and no register of who actually owns the boundary. Guardrails — if they exist at all — were bolted on after an incident: a public S3 bucket, an over-permissioned IAM role, a security group open to 0.0.0.0/0. Each fix gets applied to the account where the incident happened, not to the pattern that allowed it, so the same class of finding reappears somewhere else a quarter later.

The deeper problem isn't the mess itself, it's that retrofitting governance onto accounts that already run production workloads is materially harder than designing it upfront. By the time anyone tries to impose structure, IAM roles reference specific account IDs, VPC peering and Transit Gateway attachments assume the current network topology, and CI/CD pipelines have hardcoded account numbers and role ARNs. Moving an account into a new organizational unit, or attaching a preventive policy retroactively, risks breaking all three at once. Teams resist the migration not because they don't see the value, but because the blast radius of "let's fix governance now" is higher than the blast radius of leaving it alone.

The root cause is usually organizational, not technical: segmentation (deciding what an account boundary represents) and enforcement (deciding what policy attaches to it) get built by different people at different times. A cloud infrastructure team stands up the account structure during initial adoption. A security or compliance team adds guardrails later, usually reactively. Neither owns the seam between the two, so gaps accumulate there — accounts that exist outside the policy hierarchy, or guardrails written against an account structure that has since drifted.

The design principle: segmentation and enforcement as one system

The thesis worth stating plainly: an account or subscription boundary is simultaneously a blast-radius boundary and a policy-attachment point. Design one without the other and you end up with either unenforced workloads (accounts that exist but sit outside any guardrail hierarchy) or unusable isolation (accounts that are cleanly separated but can't inherit policy, so every control has to be applied by hand, account by account, forever).

This is why the account or subscription — not a resource group, not a tag, not a VPC — is the correct unit of enforcement. Accounts are hard isolation boundaries for IAM (no implicit cross-account trust), billing (cost allocation without relying on tag hygiene), networking (default-deny between accounts unless explicitly peered), and API service quotas (a runaway workload in one account doesn't throttle another). AWS Organizations describes accounts as "natural boundaries for permission, security, costs, and workloads" for exactly this reason. Tag-based segmentation, by contrast, is advisory: it depends on every team applying tags correctly, and most policy engines can't hard-block an action based on a tag that a user is free to omit or falsify at resource-creation time. A guardrail that "applies to resources tagged env:prod" is a suggestion; a guardrail attached to a prod organizational unit is a wall.

The practical deliverable for a platform engineer designing a landing zone is therefore two documents produced together, not sequentially: a hierarchy document (which accounts exist, what axis they segment on, who owns each one) and a guardrail map (which policy attaches at which level of that hierarchy, and why). Neither is complete without the other — a hierarchy with no guardrail map is just an org chart, and a guardrail map with no hierarchy has nowhere to attach.

Structuring the account or subscription hierarchy

Segmentation typically runs along a handful of axes, often combined: environment (prod vs. non-prod), business unit or product line, compliance/data-sensitivity boundary (PCI, HIPAA, a regulator-scoped workload that needs to be provably isolated), and platform-vs-workload accounts — shared services like networking, DNS, CI/CD runners, and centralized logging kept structurally separate from any account that runs application code.

The mechanism that makes this hierarchy usable rather than decorative is inheritance: AWS Organizational Units (OUs) and Azure management groups let a policy attached at a parent node apply automatically to every account or subscription nested beneath it, present or future. AWS Organizations documents grouping accounts into OUs specifically so policy can be applied once at the group level. The Azure Cloud Adoption Framework frames the equivalent split as a "platform landing zone" — the centralized governance and shared-resource foundation — versus the application landing zones that consume it, which is the same platform/workload separation expressed as a first-class architectural concept rather than a convention.

GCP's resource hierarchy maps onto the same idea with different names: the project is the workload-isolation boundary equivalent to an AWS account or Azure subscription, and folders nest into a tree the same way OUs and management groups do, with Organization Policy constraints inherited down that tree to every project beneath them. The Google Cloud Architecture Framework treats this resource-hierarchy-plus-policy-inheritance design as a landing zone concern to get right before workloads are provisioned, not a structure to retrofit later — the same sequencing argument this article makes for AWS and Azure.

A concrete top-level structure that generalizes well:

Root
├── Security (log archive, audit, GuardDuty/Security Hub delegated admin)
├── Shared Services (networking hub, DNS, CI/CD, artifact registries)
└── Workloads
    ├── BusinessUnitA
    │   ├── Prod
    │   └── NonProd
    └── BusinessUnitB
        ├── Prod
        └── NonProd

The Security and Shared Services OUs sit outside the Workloads tree deliberately. Security-critical accounts — the ones holding centralized CloudTrail logs, the delegated Security Hub administrator, break-glass credentials — need guardrails and access patterns that are stricter than anything applied to application accounts, and they need to survive even if a workload OU is later restructured or deleted. Mixing them into the workload hierarchy makes it impossible to reason about "what's the blast radius if this OU is compromised" without special-casing exceptions everywhere.

Centralizing guardrails: preventive, detective, and proactive controls

Guardrails generally split along two operational modes: preventive controls — AWS Service Control Policies (SCPs) with Deny effects, Azure Policy assignments with deny effect — block a noncompliant API call before it executes, and detective controls — AWS Config rules, Azure Policy in audit mode, Security Hub or Microsoft Defender findings — let the action happen and then flag the drift. AWS Control Tower actually names a third kind alongside these: proactive controls, which use CloudFormation hooks to check a resource against policy before it's ever provisioned, rather than intercepting the API call after the request is made the way an SCP does. For hierarchy design purposes proactive controls behave like preventive ones — both belong wherever new resources get created — but they're worth tracking separately in a guardrail map, since they catch malformed-template misconfiguration that a preventive SCP never sees because the call it would deny is never made.

Preventive (and proactive) controls belong at the OU or management-group level precisely because they need to apply to every account automatically, including ones that don't exist yet — that's what makes a landing zone durable rather than a one-time cleanup. Detective controls are better tuned per workload, because a legitimate exception (a data science account that genuinely needs a broader instance type list, a legacy workload still migrating off a deprecated region) shouldn't require rewriting an org-wide policy, just an accepted and tracked finding.

The operational tension is real: an SCP that's too broad blocks legitimate work, and teams under delivery pressure will find a way around a control that's actively costing them time — usually by requesting a blanket exception that quietly widens the guardrail for everyone. The fix isn't a looser policy, it's a documented exception process: a lightweight request path (a PR against the guardrail repo, reviewed by the platform team) that grants a scoped, time-boxed carve-out rather than a permanent policy weakening. Guardrails without an exception process don't stay strict — they get bypassed instead.

Enforcing guardrails as code: a policy attachment example

Guardrails should be defined and attached in the same infrastructure-as-code repository as the OU structure itself. Here's a minimal SCP denying disabling of CloudTrail and restricting API calls to approved regions, attached to a workload OU with Terraform:

resource "aws_organizations_policy" "baseline_guardrails" {
  name = "baseline-guardrails"
  type = "SERVICE_CONTROL_POLICY"

  content = jsonencode({
    Version = "2012-10-17"
    Statement = [
      {
        Sid    = "DenyCloudTrailDisable"
        Effect = "Deny"
        Action = [
          "cloudtrail:StopLogging",
          "cloudtrail:DeleteTrail"
        ]
        Resource = "*"
      },
      {
        Sid       = "DenyOutsideApprovedRegions"
        Effect    = "Deny"
        NotAction = ["iam:*", "organizations:*", "route53:*", "cloudfront:*", "support:*"]
        Resource  = "*"
        Condition = {
          StringNotEquals = {
            "aws:RequestedRegion" = ["us-east-1", "eu-west-1"]
          }
        }
      }
    ]
  })
}

resource "aws_organizations_policy_attachment" "attach_to_workloads_ou" {
  policy_id = aws_organizations_policy.baseline_guardrails.id
  target_id = aws_organizations_organizational_unit.workloads.id
}

Treat the NotAction list here as illustrative, not exhaustive. Region-restriction SCPs need to exempt every global service actually in use in the organization — budgets, KMS, WAF, STS, Trusted Advisor, Global Accelerator, and others commonly get added alongside the ones shown here — before this is attached broadly. Attaching a trimmed-down version of this policy org-wide is a fast way to break an unrelated global service the first time someone depends on it; work from AWS's own published SCP examples for a fuller exclusion list rather than copying this snippet as a complete baseline.

The reason this must live alongside the OU definition rather than in a separate "security" repo is structural: if account creation and guardrail attachment are decoupled, there will always be a window — sometimes minutes, sometimes permanently, if the second PR never lands — where a new account exists without its baseline policy. Keeping both in one module means the account can't exist without its guardrails — they're created in the same change, so there's no window where one exists without the other. It also means every guardrail change goes through the same PR review, plan, and apply pipeline as any other infrastructure change, which gives compliance an actual audit trail — who changed which guardrail, when, and why — instead of a policy that was edited by hand in a console during an incident and never recorded anywhere.

Automating account provisioning so guardrails are inherited, not applied by hand

The account-vending pattern turns "create an account" into a pipeline rather than a console click: a request triggers automation that creates the account inside the correct OU or management group, attaches the baseline guardrails from that OU by inheritance, wires up shared logging and network connectivity, and only then hands access to the requesting team. Nothing about compliance is applied after the fact — it's a precondition of the account existing.

AWS Control Tower's Account Factory and the AWS Landing Zone Accelerator implement this as opinionated, managed tooling — Control Tower explicitly targets environments with "more than a handful of accounts" that need an orchestration layer for both provisioning and ongoing compliance monitoring, while the Landing Zone Accelerator is built for organizations with more complex, multi-framework compliance requirements across a larger number of AWS services. Azure's equivalent is the Cloud Adoption Framework's landing zone guidance, which separates the platform landing zone (built once, governs everything) from application landing zones vended against it. The tradeoff against a custom Terraform module doing the same job is opinionation versus control: managed tooling gets you a working baseline fast and keeps pace with provider best practices, but a custom module lets you encode organization-specific rules the managed tool doesn't anticipate, at the cost of maintaining it yourselves.

Whichever path is chosen, the operating rule is the same: manual account creation is the most common way governance gaps reappear. Any account that exists outside the vending pipeline is, by definition, not guaranteed to have the baseline guardrails, because nothing forced them to be attached.

Preserving team autonomy inside the guardrails

Centralizing enforcement doesn't mean centralizing every deployment decision. Inside their own account, a team can reasonably hold full administrative rights — the isolation the account boundary provides means their mistakes don't propagate outward. What they shouldn't be able to do is loosen the guardrails attached above their account in the hierarchy; a delegated admin role scoped with permission boundaries (or the Azure equivalent, RBAC scoped below the management-group assignments) lets a team manage IAM within their account without being able to touch the SCP or Azure Policy assignments that apply to it from the OU level.

Self-service patterns compound this: a service catalog of pre-approved Terraform modules or CloudFormation products lets a team provision a database, a queue, or a compute cluster without filing a ticket to a central platform team, because the module itself is already guardrail-compliant by construction. This is the actual point of the exercise — guardrails succeed when the compliant path is also the fastest path, not when compliance is a manual review gate bolted onto every deploy. A landing zone that only ever says "no" trains teams to route around it; one that makes the approved path the path of least resistance doesn't need to.

Migrating an existing sprawl without a rebuild

None of this requires tearing down an existing account structure and starting over. The workable sequence is incremental: stand up the new OU or management-group hierarchy and its guardrails first, entirely separate from the accounts currently in production, then migrate accounts into it one at a time, starting with the lowest-risk, lowest-traffic workloads to validate the process before touching anything that matters.

During this coexistence period, run new guardrails in detective/audit-only mode against legacy accounts before switching to preventive enforcement. An account built under looser assumptions — a role with wildcard permissions it actually depends on, a resource deployed outside the newly approved regions — will break instantly under a preventive SCP that was designed for accounts built correctly from day one. Audit mode surfaces exactly what would break, giving the team time to remediate before the control goes live, rather than finding out via a failed deploy in the middle of an incident.

The throughline across all of this is the same one from the opening: a landing zone is defensible governance infrastructure only when segmentation and enforcement are designed as a single system from the start. Migrating sprawl into that system afterward is legitimate and often necessary, but it's still retrofitting — the goal of getting the hierarchy and the guardrail map right together is to make sure the next account, and the one after that, never needs the same retrofit.

What to do next

Start by writing down the two deliverables, even before touching Terraform: a one-page hierarchy document naming every account/OU boundary and its owner, and a guardrail map listing what policy attaches at each level and why. Audit the current account list against that map — every account that doesn't fit anywhere is the first migration candidate. If no account-vending pipeline exists yet, build the smallest possible version (a single Terraform module that creates an account, places it in an OU, and attaches baseline SCPs) before adding anything else, and route every new account request through it starting immediately, even while legacy accounts are still being migrated.
