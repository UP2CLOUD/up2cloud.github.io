AWS vs Azure vs GCP Well-Architected: A Translation Layer

Cesar A. Nogueira
April 22, 2025 · 11 min read

One trade-off conversation, three different formalizations

Every cloud platform team eventually has the same argument: do we spend more to survive a regional failure, or accept the risk and bank the savings? Do we harden this service against every threat model, or ship it and iterate? AWS, Azure and Google Cloud all agree the argument is worth having formally, and each has published a "well-architected" framework to structure it. That agreement is where the similarity ends.

Teams running workloads across more than one of these providers tend to assume the frameworks are interchangeable — that a Reliability pillar review on AWS produces the same kind of artifact as a Reliability discussion on Azure, and that GCP has a matching pillar tucked away somewhere in its docs. It doesn't hold up. The AWS Well-Architected Framework is a review methodology backed by a purpose-built tool that produces scored, remediable findings. The Azure Well-Architected Framework is explicitly framed as a set of tenets you balance against each other, with lighter continuous tooling. Google Cloud's well-architected content lives inside a broader Architecture Center as design guidance, without a dedicated review product wrapped around it. Same vocabulary, three different mechanics — which means a platform team standardizing across clouds has to build its own translation layer rather than adopt any one vendor's structure wholesale. The rest of this piece maps out where the frameworks actually converge, where they silently diverge, and how to encode a provider-neutral rubric as infrastructure rather than as another wiki page nobody reads.

AWS Well-Architected: a structured review process with tooling behind it

AWS organizes its framework around six pillars — Operational Excellence, Security, Reliability, Performance Efficiency, Cost Optimization, and Sustainability — each expressed as a set of foundational questions you answer against a specific, named workload. Per AWS's own framing, the review is explicitly "a constructive conversation about architectural decisions, and is not an audit mechanism," but in practice it behaves like a structured audit because of the tooling wrapped around it: the AWS Well-Architected Tool lets you register a workload, answer the pillar questions, and get back risk-rated findings (high risk, medium risk) tied to specific best-practice references.

Two mechanics matter for platform teams here. First, reviews are workload-scoped, not account- or org-scoped — you pick a bounded system (a service, an application) and review it in isolation, which forces decomposition before you can even start. Second, "lenses" act as vertical overlays on top of the six horizontal pillars: a Serverless Lens or SaaS Lens adds domain-specific questions without replacing the base framework. The practical payoff is that a completed AWS review produces a durable artifact — a saved workload with dated findings and risk levels — that plugs directly into a ticketing system or governance backlog. That artifact-centric design is the single most exportable idea from AWS's approach, and it's the one this piece leans on later for the cross-cloud tagging pattern.

Azure Well-Architected: pillars as tenets you actively balance

Azure's five pillars — Reliability, Security, Cost Optimization, Operational Excellence, and Performance Efficiency — cover almost the same ground as AWS's six, minus a standalone Sustainability pillar; Sustainability doesn't appear in the current five-pillar list at all. The structural difference is in how Microsoft frames the relationship between pillars. The Azure pillars documentation presents them as a matrix — pillar, workload concern, principles to apply, and, explicitly, how to "strike a balance" — rather than a checklist to clear sequentially. The framing is that pillars are naturally in tension (more reliability usually costs more; more security usually costs performance), and the exercise is trading them off deliberately rather than maximizing each independently.

That framing changes where the review work happens. Azure supplies a Well-Architected Review assessment and leans on Azure Advisor for continuous, always-on recommendations, but neither produces the same kind of discrete, versioned, risk-scored artifact that the AWS Well-Architected Tool does. The practical implication is that Azure pushes trade-off reasoning into ongoing design discussions and architecture decision records rather than a scheduled review milestone. That's not worse — for teams that already run rigorous design review processes, it fits naturally — but it means you can't assume an Azure workload has an equivalent "saved review" sitting somewhere the way an AWS workload might. If your governance process depends on a point-in-time artifact for audit purposes, Azure's continuous-tenet model requires you to manufacture that checkpoint yourself.

GCP: architecture guidance embedded in the broader Architecture Center, not a standalone framework

Google Cloud is the outlier, but not in the way it's sometimes described. Its well-architected content is not a separately branded product with its own review tool or assessment — it's a section of the Cloud Architecture Center, sitting alongside reference architectures, migration guides, and design patterns for specific technology areas (AI/ML, data analytics, networking, and so on). There's no AWS-style scored tool output and no Azure-style tenet-balancing matrix presented as the front door. Design principles are organized around named pillars — System design, Operational excellence, Security privacy and compliance, Reliability, Cost optimization, and Performance optimization, repeated consistently across the framework's pages — but unlike AWS and Azure, no product or scored review sits on top of them.

This isn't necessarily a maturity gap; it reflects a different information architecture. Google folds well-architected thinking into the same corpus as its reference architectures, so guidance is contextual to the workload pattern you're reading about rather than abstracted into a standalone framework you review against later. The practical implication for platform teams is that GCP gives you materially less tooling to build a review process on top of, even though the pillar names themselves are there to borrow. There's no equivalent of registering a workload and getting risk-rated findings back, and no equivalent of Advisor surfacing continuous recommendations tied to those pillar names. If a workload runs primarily on GCP, the team has to build its own review cadence, its own findings taxonomy, and its own remediation workflow from scratch — the pillar vocabulary is there, but the tooling and cadence around it are not.

Where the pillars actually line up — and where they silently diverge

Reliability and Security are the closest thing to a 1:1 mapping across all three providers — every framework treats resiliency/availability/recovery and threat protection/identity/data protection as first-class, universal concerns. But the evidence each framework expects differs sharply: AWS produces risk-rated findings from its tool, Azure expects you to work through a checklist-style assessment and reconcile it against Advisor recommendations, and GCP gives you prose design principles with no scoring mechanism at all. If your compliance or audit process expects a specific artifact type, "we did a well-architected review" means three different things depending on which cloud produced it.

Cost is where the divergence is about tooling rather than naming. AWS's Cost Optimization pillar is a first-class citizen of the Well-Architected Tool review, with its own risk-rated questions. Azure's Cost Optimization pillar exists in the tenet matrix and is reinforced continuously through Advisor's cost recommendations. GCP's Architecture Framework also names a Cost Optimization pillar, but it's backed by prose guidance rather than a scored tool or an Advisor-style feed of continuous recommendations — you can read the principles, but nothing registers a workload and hands back a cost-risk score the way AWS's tool does. A team trying to build one unified cost rubric across all three clouds will find that all three nominally own the topic, but only two back it with a mechanism that produces an ongoing signal rather than a one-time read.

Sustainability shows the same pattern in miniature: it's a named, dedicated pillar in AWS's six-pillar model, present as supplementary guidance in Azure's ecosystem without being one of the current five core pillars, and treated by GCP as cross-cutting guidance rather than a pillar at all. If an internal rubric assumes sustainability is a peer dimension to reliability and cost across all clouds, that assumption breaks the moment a GCP-hosted workload enters the picture — there's no equivalent artifact to point to.

Building a shared trade-off rubric as code: a tagging example

Because none of the three frameworks share a review mechanism, the most durable way to make pillar trade-offs auditable across a multi-cloud estate is to stop relying on any provider's review tool as the source of truth and instead encode the trade-off metadata directly on the resources. A mandatory tagging/labeling schema — cost-center, environment, data-classification, reliability-tier — enforced at the infrastructure-as-code layer gives you a queryable substrate that exists independently of whether the workload happens to sit on AWS, Azure, or GCP.

Terraform variable validation is a practical enforcement point, since it fails the plan before anything gets created rather than relying on a post-hoc policy scan:

variable "required_tags" {
  description = "Mandatory trade-off metadata for every workload resource"
  type = object({
    cost_center         = string
    environment         = string
    data_classification = string
    reliability_tier    = string
  })

  validation {
    condition     = contains(["public", "internal", "confidential", "restricted"], var.required_tags.data_classification)
    error_message = "data_classification must be one of: public, internal, confidential, restricted."
  }

  validation {
    condition     = contains(["tier-0", "tier-1", "tier-2", "tier-3"], var.required_tags.reliability_tier)
    error_message = "reliability_tier must be one of: tier-0, tier-1, tier-2, tier-3."
  }
}

# GCP labels are stricter than AWS/Azure tags: values must be lowercase and
# limited to letters, digits, underscores, and hyphens. Normalize before use
# instead of assigning required_tags to labels directly.
locals {
  gcp_labels = {
    for k, v in var.required_tags : k => lower(replace(v, "/[^a-zA-Z0-9_-]/", "-"))
  }
}

resource "aws_instance" "app" {
  # ...
  tags = var.required_tags
}

resource "azurerm_resource_group" "app" {
  # ...
  tags = var.required_tags
}

resource "google_compute_instance" "app" {
  # ...
  labels = local.gcp_labels
}

The same required_tags object flows into aws_instance.tags and azurerm_resource_group.tags unchanged, and into google_compute_instance.labels through the local.gcp_labels transform above — the field names and values are shared in intent, but GCP's stricter label constraints mean the GCP resource can't consume the raw required_tags object the way AWS and Azure can. A value like environment = "Production" satisfies the object's string typing and applies cleanly on AWS and Azure, but the GCP API rejects it unless it's normalized first. reliability_tier becomes the provider-agnostic stand-in for whatever each cloud's Reliability pillar independently assesses; cost_center becomes the join key for cost reporting regardless of which cloud's cost pillar backs it, scored tool or not. A platform team's own review process — a script, a dashboard, a Sentinel or OPA policy — queries this tagging layer directly instead of trying to reconcile three incompatible review-tool outputs. The tags become the translation layer; the CSP frameworks become optional inputs into filling them in correctly.

FinOps sits beside these frameworks, not inside any one of them

It's tempting to treat AWS's Cost Optimization pillar or Azure's cost-focused Advisor recommendations as "our FinOps program," but they cover a narrower slice than what the FinOps Framework actually defines. The CSP cost pillars are architectural cost hygiene — rightsizing instances, flagging idle resources, surfacing reserved-capacity or committed-use opportunities. FinOps as the FinOps Foundation defines it is a full operating lifecycle — Inform, Optimize, Operate — with personas, maturity stages, and cross-functional accountability spanning engineering, finance, and procurement, not just architecture-time recommendations.

GCP's Cost Optimization pillar makes this gap easy to underrate in the opposite direction: because it's prose guidance with no scored tool behind it, there's no CSP artifact that could plausibly be mistaken for a FinOps program in the first place, so the practice has to be imported wholesale rather than adapted from vendor guidance. On AWS and Azure the risk runs the other way — clearing the Cost Optimization pillar's findings in a Well-Architected review does not mean an organization has an Inform phase with accurate cost allocation, an Optimize phase with negotiated commitments, or an Operate phase with automated governance, even though the scored output can make it feel like more progress than it is. Assign FinOps ownership as its own practice, staffed and measured independently of whichever CSP's well-architected artifact a given workload happens to produce. Treat the CSP cost pillar findings as one input feed into that practice, not as the practice itself.

Standardizing one internal review process across three frameworks

The fix for a multi-cloud platform team isn't picking a favorite among the three frameworks and forcing every workload through it — AWS's tool doesn't run against Azure resources, and Azure's tenet matrix doesn't have an equivalent enforcement mechanism on GCP. The fix is defining an internal pillar taxonomy, five or six dimensions the org actually cares about, and mapping each CSP's native framework into it as a translation rather than a replacement.

Assign cadence and ownership per dimension rather than per cloud: reliability reviews on a quarterly cycle per workload regardless of provider, security reviews triggered by change events (new data classification, new external endpoint, dependency upgrade) rather than a calendar, and cost reviews running continuously off the tagging substrate and FinOps practice described above rather than waiting for a review milestone at all. Where a workload happens to run on AWS, feed the Well-Architected Tool's risk findings into the reliability and security dimensions of that internal taxonomy. Where it runs on Azure, feed Advisor recommendations and Well-Architected Review assessment results into the same dimensions. Where it runs on GCP, the Architecture Center's design principles become the input, filtered manually since there's no scored output to ingest automatically.

The pillars — reliability, security, cost, performance, sustainability — are a shared vocabulary across AWS, Azure, and GCP. They are not a shared mechanism, and treating them as one leads teams to assume parity that doesn't exist at review time, at audit time, or at tooling-integration time. Building and owning the translation layer between them is platform engineering work, not something any single provider's documentation will hand over pre-built.

What to do next

  • Pick the five or six pillar names your organization will standardize on internally, and write down explicitly which native artifact (AWS WA Tool finding, Azure Advisor recommendation, GCP Architecture Center principle) feeds each one.
  • Add the required_tags/labels variable pattern above to your existing Terraform modules for at least one resource type per cloud, and wire a policy check (OPA, Sentinel, or a CI script) that fails plans missing the schema.
  • Audit whether your current "FinOps program" is actually just the AWS or Azure cost pillar's recommendations rerouted to a dashboard — if so, scope out the Inform/Optimize/Operate phases you're missing against the FinOps Framework directly.
  • For any GCP-hosted workload, schedule a deliberate internal review checkpoint on the calendar — nothing in GCP's tooling will prompt you to do this the way AWS's saved-workload reviews or Azure's Advisor nudges will.
