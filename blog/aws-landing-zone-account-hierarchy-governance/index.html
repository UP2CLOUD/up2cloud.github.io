Landing Zone Design: Account Hierarchy and Guardrails

Cesar A. Nogueira
April 22, 2025 · 10 min read

Why the account hierarchy is the hardest thing to change later

Everything else in a landing zone — network topology, tagging schemes, individual policy statements — can be adjusted with a Terraform apply and a maintenance window. The account and organizational-unit (OU) hierarchy cannot. An AWS account is the hard isolation boundary for billing, IAM trust, service quotas, and blast radius: it's the one construct in AWS Organizations that resources cannot silently cross. When a workload lands in the wrong account or the wrong OU, moving it later is not a configuration change, it's a migration project — new account, new VPC, data transfer, DNS cutover, IAM re-papering, and a change window that somebody has to justify to the business.

This is precisely the point AWS Organizations makes about accounts being "natural boundaries for permission, security, costs, and workloads" — the boundary is a feature, not friction, but it means the decision about which boundary a workload sits behind has to be made correctly the first time. AWS Control Tower reinforces the same asymmetry from the tooling side: it is built to provision a landing zone and vend accounts into a structure decided in advance, not to reorganize an existing fleet of accounts into a new one. There is no Control Tower operation that reparents a live production account with running workloads into a different OU without operational consequences — SCP inheritance changes instantly, and if the new OU's guardrails are stricter, things can break the moment the account moves.

The practical framing that separates landing zones that age well from ones that get rebuilt in year two: treat the OU hierarchy and the account-per-workload boundary as expensive, upfront, mostly-irreversible decisions, and treat everything hung off that structure — SCP statement content, tag policies, individual Config rules — as a set of tunable policies you should expect to iterate on constantly. Spend the design time where it's actually load-bearing.

Control Tower versus building directly on Organizations and SCPs

Control Tower is Organizations plus opinion. Under the hood it orchestrates AWS Organizations, AWS Service Catalog, and AWS IAM Identity Center to stand up a landing zone in under an hour: a pre-built OU skeleton (typically Security and Sandbox OUs), two shared accounts for centralized logging and auditing — Log Archive and Audit — Account Factory for self-service account provisioning, centralized CloudTrail and Config aggregation into the Log Archive account, and a baseline set of preventive and detective controls (guardrails) attached at the OU level. If an organization is starting from zero and has no existing OU structure to reconcile, this is a legitimate multi-week head start.

What it costs is customization. Control Tower expects to own certain guardrails and drift detection on the core OUs and shared accounts it creates, and it periodically requires landing zone updates to adopt new mandatory controls; platform teams don't get to fully decouple that cadence from their own change windows. Account Factory's default account template is reasonably prescriptive; teams that need custom account bootstrapping (specific VPC peering at creation, non-standard IAM boundaries, integration with an external CMDB) end up either extending Account Factory for Terraform (AFT) or working around it.

Building directly on Organizations, SCPs, and hand-rolled Terraform makes sense when the organization already has a non-trivial OU structure that predates Control Tower adoption, when governance needs to span multiple tools (a third-party CSPM, a homegrown account-vending pipeline, a compliance system that doesn't integrate with Control Tower's control catalog), or when the platform team wants full control over the account creation workflow rather than working through Account Factory's abstraction. The decision reduces to a trade: Control Tower exchanges flexibility for velocity and a maintained baseline; raw Organizations plus custom IaC exchanges velocity for total control over every guardrail and every account attribute. Neither is more "correct" — the wrong choice is picking Control Tower and then fighting its opinions for two years, or building a bespoke Organizations stack that quietly reimplements half of what Control Tower already ships.

Designing the OU hierarchy: the axis you choose determines everything downstream

The single highest-leverage decision in landing zone design is which axis the OU tree is organized around, because SCPs inherit down the tree and the axis determines how cleanly a guardrail can later be scoped to the accounts that actually need it.

Three patterns cover most real deployments:

  • Environment-first: top-level OUs for Production and NonProduction, with business units or applications nested underneath. This makes it trivial to attach a strict "deny risky actions" SCP once at the Production OU and have it apply uniformly regardless of which team owns the workload.
  • Business-unit-first: top-level OUs per business unit or product line, with environment nested underneath each. This suits organizations where business units have genuinely different compliance regimes (e.g., one BU is PCI-scoped, another isn't) and need independent guardrail sets.
  • Function-first: a Security/Infrastructure/Workloads split, common in the Control Tower default skeleton, where Security holds Log Archive and Audit, Infrastructure holds shared services (networking hubs, CI/CD), and Workloads holds everything else, further subdivided by environment or business unit.

The trap is mixing axes without intent — business unit at the top, environment nested inconsistently below it, with some BUs skipping environment separation and dumping prod and dev in the same OU because "there are only three accounts." That inconsistency is exactly what makes SCP scoping unreliable two years later: a guardrail meant for "all production" now has to be attached individually to N different sub-OUs instead of once. The safer default is to pick environment as the top-level axis for any SCP that encodes a risk tolerance (region restriction, root user lockdown, prohibited services), because "risk tolerance changes by environment" is true almost everywhere, while "risk tolerance changes by business unit" is true only in specific regulatory contexts. Keep the tree shallow — three or four levels is a smell, not a feature — because every additional level is another place inheritance can produce a guardrail nobody remembers attaching.

Guardrails as code: SCPs and Control Tower controls in practice

Guardrails split into two categories that belong in different places in the tree. Preventive controls — Service Control Policies and Control Tower's mandatory or strongly recommended controls — stop an action from happening at the IAM evaluation layer, regardless of what permissions a role has. Detective controls — AWS Config rules, Control Tower's detective controls — flag non-compliant resources after the fact for remediation. Preventive controls belong high in the tree, on OUs where the restriction is genuinely universal (region allow-lists, root account restrictions); detective controls tolerate being scoped more granularly per workload OU, since a false positive doesn't take down a deployment.

A concrete region-restriction SCP, attached at a Workloads OU, looks like this:

{
  "Version": "2012-10-17",
  "Statement": [
    {
      "Sid": "DenyOutsideApprovedRegions",
      "Effect": "Deny",
      "NotAction": [
        "iam:*",
        "organizations:*",
        "route53:*",
        "cloudfront:*",
        "waf:*",
        "wafv2:*",
        "support:*",
        "sts:*",
        "kms:*",
        "acm:*",
        "health:*",
        "budgets:*",
        "ce:*",
        "shield:*",
        "chime:*",
        "globalaccelerator:*",
        "importexport:*",
        "trustedadvisor:*"
      ],
      "Resource": "*",
      "Condition": {
        "StringNotEquals": {
          "aws:RequestedRegion": ["us-east-1", "eu-west-1"]
        }
      }
    }
  ]
}

The NotAction list exists because IAM, Organizations, Route 53, CloudFront, WAF Classic and WAFv2, STS, KMS, ACM, Health, Budgets, Cost Explorer, Shield, Chime, Global Accelerator, Import/Export, and Trusted Advisor are global, near-global, or cross-region-dependent services that don't respect regional scoping the same way; excluding them avoids breaking account bootstrapping, cross-region encryption (KMS-backed Secrets Manager, SSE-KMS replication), ACM certificate issuance in us-east-1 for CloudFront, and cost/security visibility tools like Cost Explorer, Budgets, and Shield that teams tend to forget when writing this SCP by hand. AWS Config is deliberately left out of this exclusion list: Config recorders, delivery channels, and rules are regional resources, so denying config:* outside the approved regions is the guardrail doing its job rather than something to route around — the exception is the optional cross-region aggregator, which is a separate opt-in resource, not something ordinary Config recording needs. Treat this NotAction list as a starting point, not a finished list — extend it for any other global or cross-region service the account fleet actually uses before relying on it in production.

Attach this at the OU rather than per-account so every account inherits it automatically on creation — the whole point of scoping decisions correctly at the OU level is that new accounts get the right guardrails without anyone remembering to attach them individually.

Managing Organizations, OUs, and SCPs as Terraform-managed IaC — using the aws_organizations_organizational_unit and aws_organizations_policy / aws_organizations_policy_attachment resources — gives the platform team a reviewable diff for every guardrail change and a git history explaining why a policy statement exists. This works alongside Control Tower, but with a caveat: Control Tower owns certain resources (the core OUs, the mandatory controls, the Log Archive and Audit accounts) and will drift-detect if Terraform tries to manage those same resources outside Control Tower's own APIs. The practical pattern is Terraform-managing everything downstream of the Control Tower baseline — custom OUs, workload-specific SCPs, tag policies — while leaving the Control Tower-managed core alone, or managing it through the Control Tower Terraform provider resources specifically designed for that purpose rather than raw Organizations resources.

Account vending and workload onboarding

However accounts get created — Account Factory, Account Factory for Terraform, or a custom Service Catalog product — the vending workflow is where the OU and guardrail decisions made upfront actually get enforced. Every new account should be requested with its target OU specified as a parameter, not decided after the fact; the account inherits that OU's SCPs and any Control Tower controls from the moment it's created, which is also the moment CloudTrail, Config, and the guardrail baseline should already be active.

A minimal baseline every vended account needs at creation time, before any workload touches it:

  • Centralized logging: CloudTrail delivering to the Log Archive account, Config recording enabled and aggregating centrally.
  • Tagging: mandatory tags (cost center, environment, owner) enforced via a tag policy or SCP, so FinOps allocation works from account zero rather than being retrofitted.
  • Network baseline: a VPC provisioned from a standard template if the account will host workloads, ideally attached to a shared networking hub via Transit Gateway or VPC peering rather than each account building its own internet egress path.
  • Break-glass IAM role: an emergency access role with tightly scoped, logged, alertable permissions, provisioned before any team-facing IAM roles exist.

The reason this matters for the thesis of the article: onboarding workflow design forces the OU and guardrail decisions to happen at account creation, not during workload migration. If the vending pipeline lets a requester create an account without specifying an OU, or lets guardrails be attached "later, once the workload settles," the organization has effectively deferred the hardest decision to the point where reversing it is most expensive — after data and traffic already live there.

What's cheap to reverse — and what isn't worth over-designing

The corollary to the account/OU boundary being hard to reverse is that most of what hangs off it isn't: the content of an individual SCP statement, the specific set of Config rules attached to a detective-controls OU, tag policy key names, and Account Factory template defaults for non-structural settings. These change with a Terraform apply and normal change management, because none of them move an account's position in the tree or its billing/IAM boundary. A region-restriction SCP that's slightly too permissive on day one is a follow-up PR, not a re-platforming effort — reserve that level of upfront scrutiny for the OU axis and account boundaries themselves.

A pragmatic starting checklist for landing zone design

For teams standing up a new landing zone, or auditing an existing one before it calcifies further:

  1. Start with a minimal OU structure, not a hierarchy designed for hypothetical future business units. A Security / Infrastructure / Workloads split, with environment nested under Workloads, covers most organizations for years. Add OUs when a real requirement appears, not speculatively.
  2. Decide Control Tower versus a custom Organizations build before the first non-management account exists. This decision gets exponentially more expensive once accounts, SCPs, and workloads accumulate under one model.
  3. Attach the guardrail baseline — SCPs and Control Tower controls — at OU creation, before any account is vended into that OU, not retrofitted after workloads have already onboarded.
  4. Set a review cadence for the OU tree itself — quarterly is reasonable — as account count grows, specifically checking for OUs that mix axes inconsistently or guardrails attached at the wrong level.

The account/OU boundary is a special case of a broader pattern worth carrying into any cloud architecture decision: some choices are cheap to unwind with a change request, and some aren't — and the ones that aren't deserve disproportionate design time before the first workload lands. On AWS, that decision is the account and OU hierarchy described above.

What to do next

Before writing a single SCP, sketch the OU tree on paper and stress-test it against three or four real workload scenarios the organization already has — not hypothetical ones. If a workload doesn't cleanly map to an OU in the sketch, the axis is probably wrong. Then decide Control Tower vs. custom Organizations based on whether an existing account fleet needs to be reconciled into the new structure or whether this is a genuine greenfield start. Once the structure is fixed, bring the OU tree and SCPs under Terraform management with plan/apply in CI, so every guardrail change is a reviewable diff rather than a console click nobody remembers making — and build the account-vending workflow so OU selection and guardrail attachment are mandatory, non-skippable steps at account creation, not something a workload owner requests after the fact.
