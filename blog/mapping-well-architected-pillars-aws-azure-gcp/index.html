Mapping Well-Architected Pillars: AWS, Azure, GCP

Cesar A. Nogueira
April 22, 2025 · 11 min read

Why "Well-Architected" Means Three Different Things

A platform team running production workloads on AWS and Azure — increasingly common after an acquisition, a multi-region resilience mandate, or a deliberate avoidance-of-lock-in strategy — inherits a subtle problem the moment someone says "this design passes the reliability pillar review." Passes whose reliability pillar? AWS's Well-Architected Framework, Azure's Well-Architected Framework, and Google Cloud's Architecture Framework each define their own taxonomy of what "well-architected" means, and those taxonomies are not interchangeable. They share vocabulary — reliability, security, cost, performance show up in some form in all three — but the underlying structure, the review mechanics, and even the count of top-level categories differ enough that a claim validated against one framework does not automatically transfer to another.

This matters beyond terminology. When an architecture review board evaluates a trade-off — say, accepting higher latency for lower cost — the framework in use shapes which questions get asked and which get skipped. A team that internalizes AWS's six pillars as the mental model for "good architecture" will unconsciously apply that model when reviewing an Azure workload, missing wherever Azure's pillar matrix asks a structurally different question. The reverse is equally true for teams that learned Azure first and then inherited an AWS estate.

Layered on top of all three provider frameworks is a fourth taxonomy that governs cost specifically: the FinOps Framework. It doesn't map cleanly onto any single provider's cost pillar — it defines domains and capabilities (usage understanding, unit economics, forecasting, chargeback) that partially overlap with, and partially exceed, what any CSP's "Cost Optimization" pillar covers. A team that treats a provider's cost pillar as synonymous with FinOps practice will find real gaps once a FinOps practitioner asks about unit economics or showback and the provider's checklist has no corresponding entry.

The fix isn't to pick a favorite framework and force the others into it. It's to lay out each framework's actual structure, accept that they diverge, and build an explicit mapping that a multi-cloud team can version-control and revise — which is what the rest of this article does.

AWS's Six Named Pillars

AWS's framework is built around six named pillars: Operational Excellence, Security, Reliability, Performance Efficiency, Cost Optimization, and Sustainability. Each pillar is documented independently, with its own design principles and its own set of review questions. AWS's documentation frames this with a construction metaphor — pillars supporting a structure — which is apt for the delivery mechanism: the pillars are flat and parallel, not nested or weighted against each other by the framework itself.

In practice, this plays out through the AWS Well-Architected Tool, which walks a workload through each pillar's question set independently and produces a per-pillar risk assessment. A workload can score well on Reliability and poorly on Cost Optimization without either pillar's review questions referencing the other — the tool doesn't force a trade-off conversation between pillars, it surfaces gaps within each one separately. AWS extends this base model with "lenses" — supplementary question sets for specific domains like SaaS, serverless, or machine learning workloads — layered on top of the same six pillars rather than replacing them.

The practical consequence for a mapping exercise: Cost Optimization in AWS's model is one lens among six, evaluated largely on its own terms (rightsizing, pricing models, matching supply to demand) rather than as a cross-cutting concern threaded through every other pillar's questions. That independence is convenient for a checklist but means a reliability decision doesn't automatically surface its cost pillar consequences without a human connecting the two.

Azure's Pillar Matrix and Design Principles

Azure's framework names five pillars: Reliability, Security, Cost Optimization, Operational Excellence, and Performance Efficiency. Four of those five names are near-identical to AWS's — a surface similarity that invites the exact false-equivalence this article is warning against. The delivery mechanism is where Azure actually diverges: Microsoft presents the pillars as a matrix, crossing each pillar against "workload concern," "apply the principles," and "strike a balance" columns, and explicitly frames the pillars as requiring trade-offs against one another rather than independent checklists.

That "strike a balance" framing is a structural difference from AWS's more additive, per-pillar scoring. Azure's assessment mechanism — the Azure Well-Architected Review — is workload-scoped and produces a score meant to be interpreted in the context of that specific workload's priorities, rather than a uniform checklist applied identically everywhere. Two workloads can legitimately score differently on the same pillar for the same underlying practice, because Azure's model expects the pillars to be weighted against each other per workload rather than evaluated as independent pass/fail gates.

The more consequential gap for this article's mapping: Azure has no standalone Sustainability pillar. Where AWS treats sustainability as a sixth, independently reviewed pillar, Azure folds sustainability guidance into Operational Excellence and into separate Cloud Adoption Framework material outside the Well-Architected Framework proper. Any mapping table between AWS and Azure has to record this as a real structural gap, not paper over it with a same-name assumption — because there is no "same name" to lean on here.

GCP's Architecture Framework: Domains and Lenses

Google Cloud's Architecture Framework organizes around a similar-but-not-identical set of categories: Operational Excellence, Security/Privacy/Compliance, Reliability, Cost Optimization, and Performance Optimization. Structurally, GCP's framework reads less like a scored checklist (AWS) or a workload-weighted matrix (Azure) and more like a set of design documents and best-practice recommendations organized by category, with less emphasis on a formal per-workload scoring tool as the primary artifact.

GCP layers a distinct concept on top of its core categories: "lenses," most notably an AI and ML lens, that cut across the standard categories with domain-specific guidance rather than adding new top-level pillars. This is structurally closer to AWS's lens concept than to anything in Azure's model, but it's applied within GCP's own category set, not imported wholesale.

The practical effect for cross-framework comparison: because GCP's framework is documentation-and-recommendation-driven rather than assessment-driven in the way AWS's tool or Azure's review are, a team can't assume a GCP "pass" carries the same evidentiary weight as an AWS Well-Architected Tool output or an Azure workload score. Normalizing GCP guidance into a comparable form — turning prose recommendations into the same kind of question-and-answer artifact AWS and Azure produce — is extra work a mapping exercise has to account for explicitly.

Like Azure, GCP has no standalone sustainability pillar; sustainability considerations appear folded into Operational Excellence guidance rather than named separately. That puts GCP and Azure on the same side of the sustainability-pillar gap relative to AWS, even though the two arrived at that omission through structurally different frameworks.

Where the Three Frameworks Structurally Diverge

Laid side by side, the divergence is concrete enough to tabulate:

| Dimension | AWS | Azure | GCP | |---|---|---|---| | Top-level count | 6 pillars | 5 pillars | 5 categories | | Named sustainability pillar | Yes | No (folded into Operational Excellence + CAF) | No (folded into Operational Excellence) | | Delivery mechanism | Checklist tool, per-pillar scoring | Matrix, per-workload weighted assessment | Documentation/recommendations, lens-based | | Cost treatment | Independent pillar | Pillar requiring explicit trade-off against others | Category, less formally scored | | Domain extensions | Lenses (SaaS, serverless, ML) | Workload-specific guidance | Lenses (AI/ML) |

The thesis-critical point this table makes visible: identical or near-identical pillar names do not guarantee identical review questions. "Reliability" in AWS's checklist model asks a self-contained set of questions scored against that workload in isolation. "Reliability" in Azure's matrix model is explicitly framed as something to be balanced against the other four pillars, with the review mechanism itself asking where the trade-offs were made. Treating "Reliability passed" as a portable fact between the two providers silently drops that context.

This is exactly why a naive one-to-one pillar mapping — Reliability↔Reliability, Cost Optimization↔Cost Optimization, done — fails. It captures the label but not the review mechanics, the scoring model, or (for sustainability) the mere existence of a comparable category. An explicit, maintained mapping has to record all three: the nearest-equivalent pillar/category, the mechanism difference, and any structural gap.

Layering the FinOps Framework's Domains on Top

The FinOps Framework defines its own taxonomy of domains and capabilities — organized around phases (Inform, Optimize, Operate), personas, and capability areas like understanding cost and usage, quantifying business value, and optimizing usage and cost — that exists independently of any single cloud provider's model. It was built to be provider-agnostic by design, which is precisely why it doesn't slot neatly under any one CSP's Cost Optimization pillar.

Each provider's single Cost Optimization pillar tends to map to only a subset of FinOps capabilities — largely the parts concerned with rate optimization (commitment discounts, reserved capacity) and usage optimization (rightsizing, eliminating waste). What a CSP's cost pillar checklist typically does not cover, because it sits outside any individual provider's scope, includes FinOps-specific practices like unit economics (cost per customer, per transaction, per feature), forecasting and budget variance analysis, and chargeback/showback allocation across business units. Those practices are inherently cross-provider and cross-account by nature — a unit economics model spanning an AWS-hosted service and an Azure-hosted service can't live inside either provider's pillar definition.

The conclusion that follows directly: the FinOps Framework has to be layered on top of whichever CSP pillar mapping a team builds, not substituted for the provider's own cost pillar, and not assumed to be fully covered by it either. A cost review that only checks the AWS or Azure Cost Optimization pillar and calls it done will miss the FinOps capabilities that live outside any single provider's checklist.

Building an Explicit Cross-Framework Mapping (Config Example)

The practical fix is a version-controlled mapping artifact the platform team owns and updates, rather than tribal knowledge that lives in one architect's head. A YAML file works well because it's diffable in pull requests and easy to validate in CI:

mappings:
  - aws_pillar: reliability
    azure_pillar: reliability
    gcp_category: reliability
    finops_domain: quantify-business-value
    finops_capability: unit-economics
    notes: >
      Azure frames reliability as a balance against cost and performance
      in the same review pass; AWS scores it independently. When citing
      this mapping in an ADR, state which provider's mechanics produced
      the score.

  - aws_pillar: cost-optimization
    azure_pillar: cost-optimization
    gcp_category: cost-optimization
    finops_domain: optimize-usage-and-cost
    finops_capability: rate-optimization
    notes: >
      Covers commitment discounts and rightsizing only. Unit economics,
      forecasting, and chargeback/showback are FinOps capabilities with
      no equivalent CSP pillar entry — must be reviewed separately.

  - aws_pillar: sustainability
    azure_pillar: null
    gcp_category: null
    finops_domain: null
    finops_capability: null
    notes: >
      No Azure or GCP standalone equivalent. Azure guidance lives in
      Operational Excellence and the Cloud Adoption Framework; GCP
      guidance lives in Operational Excellence recommendations.

An architecture decision record (ADR) template can enforce use of this mapping rather than leaving it optional. A minimal validation script, run in CI against ADR front matter, can require that any ADR touching a cross-cloud trade-off cite a mapping_id that resolves to an entry in the YAML file:

const mapping = require('./cross-framework-mapping.json');
const adr = require('./adr-front-matter.json');

const entry = mapping.mappings.find(m => m.aws_pillar === adr.aws_pillar);
if (!entry) {
  throw new Error(`ADR references unmapped AWS pillar: ${adr.aws_pillar}`);
}
if (!adr.finops_capability_reviewed) {
  throw new Error('ADR must confirm the mapped FinOps capability was reviewed');
}

This artifact isn't a one-time deliverable. AWS revises pillar guidance around re:Invent, Azure periodically updates its Well-Architected Review content, and GCP's Architecture Framework is updated on its own cadence — pillar definitions and review questions are not static. The mapping file needs an owner and a review trigger tied to those release cycles, not a "write once" assumption.

Applying the Mapping to a Reliability-vs-Cost Trade-off

Consider a concrete decision: a workload needs a resilience posture, and the three providers offer structurally different building blocks for it — AWS multi-AZ deployment, Azure availability-zone-redundant configuration, and GCP multi-region deployment. Each provider's reliability guidance implies a different cost pillar consequence, and without the mapping, a team evaluates each in isolation using that provider's own mental model.

Under AWS's framework, the Reliability pillar's review questions about withstanding component failure will point toward multi-AZ redundancy, and the Cost Optimization pillar separately evaluates whether that redundancy is right-sized — the two pillars are reviewed independently, so the cost implication of the reliability choice has to be manually carried over by whoever runs both reviews. Under Azure's matrix framing, the same reliability decision is reviewed with cost as an explicit counterweight in the same pass — the "strike a balance" language in Azure's pillar matrix means the review itself asks what the availability-zone-redundant configuration costs relative to a cheaper single-zone option. GCP's multi-region guidance, delivered as design-document recommendations rather than a scored checklist, requires the reviewer to extract the cost implication from prose rather than a paired pillar score.

Using the mapping from the previous section, all three decisions route to the same FinOps capability — unit economics under Quantify Business Value — regardless of which provider's reliability pillar produced the recommendation. That's the payoff: the architecture review can ask "what does this redundancy choice do to cost per transaction" as a single, consistent question across all three clouds, instead of getting three differently-shaped answers depending on which provider's review mechanics happened to be in use. Without the explicit mapping, teams default to whichever provider's mental model they learned first, or make the reliability-cost trade-off implicitly and inconsistently between providers — exactly the risk this article opened with.

Operationalizing the Mapping Across a Multi-Cloud Team

Turning this from a one-off document into a working practice takes a few concrete moves. Assign the mapping file to the platform team as an owned artifact, not a shared wiki page nobody updates — someone's name should be on the pull requests that revise it. Require ADRs that touch cross-cloud architecture or cost trade-offs to cite a specific mapping entry, enforced in CI the way the validation script above illustrates, so the requirement isn't just a review-checklist suggestion. Include a FinOps practitioner in the architecture review gate for any decision that maps to a FinOps domain, since the CSP pillar reviewers won't naturally surface unit economics or chargeback concerns on their own. And re-validate the mapping on a fixed cadence tied to the providers' own release cycles — after AWS re:Invent, after Azure's periodic Well-Architected content updates, and whenever GCP revises its Architecture Framework — rather than waiting for a mapping entry to be visibly wrong before touching it.

Done consistently, this turns an ad hoc, provider-by-provider review process — where each cloud gets evaluated on its own terms by whoever happens to know that provider's framework best — into a single, comparable evaluation surface. Reliability, cost, operations, performance, and sustainability trade-offs stop being three (or four, counting FinOps) separate vocabularies and become one shared one, explicitly maintained rather than assumed.
